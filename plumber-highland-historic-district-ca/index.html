The Highland Historic District is listed on the National Register of Historic Places and sits near Palm Avenue, in the oldest part of the city. The National Park Service record gives its periods of significance as 1875 to 1949. Owners of older buildings face a plumbing question that newer neighborhoods do not, which is how to work on a building when nobody has a record of what is inside the walls.

This page covers what the public record says about the district and how to find out what your own pipes are made of. To ask about a leak or a clog, call (909) 505-3225.

The Oldest Part of Highland

Highland was surveyed as a townsite in 1891 and the plat was recorded in 1893, according to the City's Land Use Element. Citrus groves shaped the early town, and the Highland Historic District holds some of its oldest buildings.

The City's Community Design Element describes the district as covering about 290 acres with 99 contributing buildings, and as containing excellent examples of early 1900s architecture. It adds that the area has both residences and early commercial buildings, and that Palm Avenue links it to the planned Town Center along Base Line.

What the Record Says About Style and Age

The National Register listing names the Bungalow and Craftsman styles and the Queen Anne style, with 1891, 1903 and 1937 as significant years. Those dates describe the buildings and the district, not the plumbing inside them.

That distinction is the practical point. A building can be a century old and still have replaced supply lines, a new drain section or a newer water heater. No public record tells you what is in your walls, so assumptions about pipe material are risky in either direction.

Old Buildings Need Evidence, Not Guesses

Pipe material depends on when a building was plumbed and what has been repaired since. A previous owner may have replaced a section after a leak. A remodel may have added new copper or plastic beside original pipe.

Before planning a repair or a repipe, gather evidence. A camera look at the drain line and a pressure test of the supply side show what is there, and they cost far less than opening walls on a guess.

Where the District Is

The National Park Service describes the district as roughly bounded by Cole and Nona Avenues and Pacific and Church Streets. The City's Land Use Element separately describes the Original Highland Townsite south of Pacific Street between Palm Avenue and Church Avenue. The map is centered on Palm Avenue, and the City's planning office can confirm whether a specific parcel falls inside the district.

How to Find Out What You Have

These four checks work for most older buildings and need no demolition.

  1. Look at the main valve and the meter

    Note the pipe material where the water enters the building. It is often the first visible clue.

  2. Check the water heater

    Read the data plate for the manufacture date and look at the supply lines above the tank.

  3. Ask for a camera look at the drain

    A recording shows the pipe material, joints, bellies and roots, with the distance from the cleanout.

  4. Ask for a pressure test

    A test on the supply side shows whether the lines hold pressure before you spend money on repairs.

Exterior Changes and the City's Preservation Rules

Chapter 16.32 of the Highland Municipal Code covers historic and cultural preservation. It says work on designated resources and on properties in historic districts requires a certificate of appropriateness from the City's board for changes such as alteration, remodeling, additions and demolition.

The same chapter says ordinary maintenance and repair that does not change design, material or exterior appearance does not need a certificate. A new vent terminal or relocated equipment on an exterior wall could change appearance, so ask City Community Development before the work starts. The chapter refers to an Old Town Historic District, so confirm whether your parcel is covered.

Services Owners of Older Buildings Ask About

Drain and sewer inspection

A repeat clog in an older building is a reason for a sewer camera inspection before anyone cleans the line again. Buyers often ask for one before closing.

Leaks in supply lines

If the meter moves with every fixture off, leak detection finds the spot with tools instead of demolition.

Whole-building piping

When tests show several failing sections, repiping becomes a planning question. Ask for the scope in writing.
